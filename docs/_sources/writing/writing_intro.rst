The Practice of Technical Writing
==================================

In almost every conversation that lasts beyond thirty minutes, the topic eventually turns to one’s professional life. The question is something along the lines of, “What do you do for a living?” When I tell my interlocutor that I am a Technical Writer, I get one of three responses: “That sounds boring.” or “So I should blame you for all the bad documentation.” or awkward silence and shift change of topic.

Technical writing is a special form of writing focused on explaining as easily as possible the operation of a product, service, device, or process to people not already familiar with the product, service, or device.

There are many writers in the world. When asked, most people would probably define a writer as a novelist, journalist, or poet. A few people might also add those who write books that popularized complex ideas for amateurs, like economics or physics. Very few would probably include technical writers, which is odd. After journalists, technical writers are probably among the most common type of professional writers.


The Merriam-Webster Dictionary offers the following definition of **professional**:[#professional]_.

 | 2 a. participating for gain or livelihood in an activity or field of endeavor often engaged in by amateurs.
 |   b. having a particular profession as a permanent career.
 |   c. engaged in by persons receiving financial return.

While people outside the discipline might not think of a technical writer as such, since they are paid to write technical writers are by definition professional writers.

People sometimes say that a specific degree or program can prepare you to be a technical writer. That assumption is found in typical job description listing for these roles. In truth, technical writing requires skills and experience in so many areas, there is not a single path anyone could pursue to prepare for the technical writing. No degree, program, certificate, or online course that prepares you for all the skills and knowledge you must have to be an effective technical writer. The possible scope of required knowledge is so vast, and the expectations are so completely varied from one project to the next that it’s impossible to know it all.

Technical Writers I’ve know, and have hired, came from a variety of educational backgrounds, including the law, mathematics, theology, molecular biology, computer science, music, business administration, chemistry, ecology, electrical engineering, accounting, and more. While technical writing does not require any particular educational background, but it does require two things: a facility with language (most often English) and unsatiated curiosity.

I’ve found that technical writers share some common traits.

- **Curiosity**:  Many technical writers I’ve worked with are deeply curious about how things work, so they love to learn. Like teaching any subject, trying to explain in writing about how things work and how to use them to a newcomer helps one to learn about the subject being discussed.

- **Prefer creativity time**: They like long periods of uninterrupted thinking and creativity. It’s not a personality trait as much as a work style preference. I’ve worked with gregarious extroverts and subdued introverts and all sorts of personality types in between. Most have been highly successful as technical writers. The common traits seem to be that once the interviews are complete, the design documents have been scoured, the user stories have properly understood, these writers like spending uninterrupted time making complex ideas, concepts, or features as clear and simply as possible.

- **Passion for reading and readers**: Technical writers who last as writers are, at heart, readers. They seem to love reading books, articles, blogs, novels, ad copy, web content and the back of cereal boxes, so they care about the people who read and use their written instructions.
